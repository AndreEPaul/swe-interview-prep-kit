# Behavioral preparation: true stories with follow-up depth

## Start from events, not values slogans

Build a small set of real examples covering delivery, ambiguity, conflict, failure, feedback, mentorship, product scoping, and technical judgment. Do not fabricate one story per value.

Start with a story or answer the candidate already brought. A requested framing explanation or draft is appropriate coaching; distinguish it from an unaided rehearsal and do not invent missing history. Keep story banks, drafts, and requested cards outside this repository in a confirmed private location. Generalize confidential employer details rather than requesting more.

For each card, record:

- Situation and the actual goal.
- Your role versus collaborators' work.
- Decision, alternatives, constraints, and actions.
- Outcome, evidence/scope, and what remains unknown.
- Reflection and an actual changed habit.

Use the [STAR template](../templates/star-card.md) and [evidence ledger](../templates/experience-ledger.md).

## Route by the prompt's qualifier

- "Initiated" is not synonymous with delivering a PM-assigned feature.
- "Received feedback" is not giving feedback or independently discovering a lesson.
- "Not your manager" can mean a peer, reviewer, designer, Product partner, or collaborator.
- "Applied feedback" requires some actual action, not only agreement and a future plan.
- "Failure" needs a real consequence or missed expectation; do not erase it with the eventual success.
- "Most complex" may mean technical difficulty, product ambiguity, or delivery coordination. Clarify if needed.

A smaller genuine event is preferable to a dramatic improvised answer.

## Give an opening answer with room to probe

Aim for roughly 1-2 minutes:

1. One or two context sentences.
2. What you personally needed to decide/do.
3. The central action and why.
4. Outcome and one reflection.

Do not announce every STAR letter or list every feature. Let follow-ups expose the depth.

## Concrete beats generic

Instead of "we collaborated on scope," explain one feature deferred and one retained:

- Both might be expensive.
- One is not essential to the user's core job.
- A partner explains why the other is essential.
- You change your recommendation and do the work.

That demonstrates product judgment more clearly than a polished claim of collaboration. Use your actual choices, not this hypothetical as a script.

## Own honest seams

- A teammate originated a prototype; your contribution may be making it dependable and adopted.
- You may own one application/workstream, not a migration or platform.
- A metric can be accurate within tests but not a universal production guarantee.
- An estimate must stay an estimate.
- Documentation and a plan can be completed while rollout results remain pending.
- A system/tool can help you while you retain diagnosis and validation responsibility.

Honest scope is a senior signal, not an admission of irrelevance.

## Feedback and failure without defensiveness

For feedback:

> observed behavior -> initial reaction -> what you understood -> specific change -> what happened next.

Do not invent instant agreement. Explain how you came to accept a useful critique. A recent ongoing example can be used honestly, but choose another if the question requires established impact.

For failure:

> your assumption/action -> consequence -> correction -> changed behavior.

Keep the failure meaningful rather than turning it into "I worked too hard and saved everything." Give credit and avoid blaming colleagues.

## Handling challenge

If an interviewer says the project does not sound complex:

- Stay curious about which kind of difficulty they want to explore.
- Describe one real constraint, alternative, and decision.
- Do not insist the feature is technically revolutionary.
- Use another project if it better answers the actual question.

The interviewer's challenge is not proof of hostility or a rejection. Strong final outcomes cannot be inferred from rapport either.

## Use a culture/values document naturally

Read the current version and distinguish stated values from interview instructions. Connect it to real actions: seeking dissent, taking responsibility, revising a decision, giving feedback, learning from a miss.

Behavior first, vocabulary second. Do not retrofit every story to every principle, rehearse fake passion, or pretend ordinary alignment was a formal decision process you never used.

Constructive optimism does not mean suppressing concerns or agreeing with everyone.

## AI-fluency discussions

If relevant to the role, explain:

> problem -> your judgment -> tool contribution -> verification/permissions -> outcome -> learning.

Building agent tooling, writing domain procedures, using an assistant to investigate, and training a model are different contributions. Be precise.

Prefer one real example you can explain in depth over a list of tools. Distinguish a prototype, smoke test, controlled evaluation, and production outcome.

## Small refresh, not a new identity

Choose two stories, ask one counterfactual or depth question on each, then stop. Across separate interviewers, reuse can be sensible. Within one conversation, use a different beat or story rather than identical retelling; novelty is not more important than relevance.

Prepare genuine career/role motivation and a few team questions. Do not let compensation or a recognizable brand become the only articulated reason you want the work.
