# Resume coaching: make true impact easy to understand

## What this guide generalizes

Useful experienced-reviewer feedback often concerns clarity, selection, and credibility rather than fancy wording: outcome-first bullets, a tighter summary, contextualized metrics, relevant technical breadth, and accurate ownership.

Earlier resume versions add another perspective: how responsibilities became evidence, how the target narrative changed, and what useful facts were lost or overstated during revision. External feedback and longitudinal review complement each other.

This is a method, not a quotation from a particular reviewer or a guarantee about applicant-tracking systems. Begin with the actual current resume and target roles. A resume is evidence of what a draft says, not independent verification of its claims.

## Optional: learn from earlier versions

Old versions are useful inputs, not a prerequisite or a reason to postpone improving the current resume.

1. Confirm which document is current and the target role. Order supplied versions by the full date, including the year, not filename sorting or modification time; ask about ambiguous dates.
2. Compare the earliest useful draft, major intervening revisions, and the current export. If reviewing every supplied version, record minor or unchanged revisions rather than inventing a milestone for each file.
3. Track the same projects across versions. Separate newly gained experience, newly surfaced evidence of older work, changed emphasis/selection, factual corrections, wording, and layout. First appearance does not establish a project's completion date.
4. Record what improved and what was lost. Shorter wording may drop a relevant language, personal contribution, metric baseline, or qualification such as pilot, agreed scope, or pre-launch testing.
5. Reconcile conflicts with the candidate and supporting evidence. Newest is not automatically most accurate; repetition across drafts is not verification. Moving a bullet under a different employer or role changes its implied attribution even if the words stay the same.
6. Preserve a brief private rationale: version/date, intended role if known, material change, supporting evidence, unresolved fact, and keep/revise/restore decision. If the reason for an old edit is unavailable, label your interpretation rather than inventing it.

Look for the larger progression: a list of responsibilities becoming concrete contribution, a project inventory becoming a coherent narrative, or previously hidden technical breadth becoming visible. Do not merge every old bullet into the current resume or impose the same target narrative on every role.

Keep personal documents, reviewer identities, and actual before/after bullets private unless separately approved for sharing. Generalize the lesson; label synthetic examples. Resume revisions alone do not establish why interview results changed, and later-round performance is not the same measure as resume-to-screen conversion.

## 1. Build an evidence ledger before rewriting

For each project, record:

- The user/business problem and why it mattered.
- Your personal role and the team's broader scope.
- Decisions/actions you can explain under follow-up.
- Outcome, baseline, population/time period, and source.
- Whether a figure is measured, estimated, or unknown.
- What you did not own, and what is confidential.

Use the [ledger template](../templates/experience-ledger.md). If a fact is missing, ask. Do not fill the gap with a plausible metric.

## 2. Choose a coherent target narrative

A summary should answer what kind of engineer you are and what value you bring. It should not be a compressed inventory of every project, adjective, and technology.

- Align evidence with the actual role; do not claim new experience to match keywords.
- Represent breadth without implying equal depth in every stack.
- Avoid accidentally positioning a product engineer as a specialist in an unrelated niche merely because recent AI/tooling work dominates the text.
- Preserve real relevant earlier language or backend work when it matters to the role.

## 3. Shape bullets around impact and decisions

Useful structure:

> Outcome or useful capability -> personal action/technical mechanism -> scope or constraint.

Not every bullet needs a number. A verified capability, deadline met, failure mode removed, or manual step eliminated can be meaningful.

**Synthetic example, not a candidate claim:**

- Weak: "Worked on a dashboard using React and several APIs."
- Better: "Built a self-service investigation view so support staff could inspect record changes without manual log queries; coordinated API metadata and deep-linkable filters in React."
- If a valid measured result exists, add its baseline/scope. Do not invent time saved or support-ticket reduction merely to make the sentence look stronger.

## 4. Audit attribution

Check each verb:

- "Built" which part?
- "Led" technical decisions, people, delivery, or the whole program?
- "Initiated" the product idea, or an implementation improvement?
- "Scaled" the team's system to a number, or personally wrote that many components?
- "Reduced failures to zero" in which environment and tested population?
- "Delivered" the entire system or an explicitly negotiated MVP?
- "Improved" an observed outcome, or enabled a capability intended to help?

Co-leading and productionalizing someone else's prototype are legitimate achievements. They do not need sole-origin embellishment.

Tie each achievement to its actual employer, role, and work period, especially when regrouping bullets to save space. A more polished sentence that removes a material scope qualifier is not an improvement.

## 5. Make it legible to an outsider

- Replace internal names/acronyms with an understandable product purpose.
- Show why the technical work mattered before listing implementation detail.
- Keep dates, promotions, acquisitions, and employer transitions accurate.
- Avoid unsupported "industry-leading," "revolutionized," or "expert in" claims.
- Choose concise, natural phrasing rather than layers of compounds and adjectives.
- Use a length/layout appropriate to career and market; one page is not a universal rule.

## 6. Review in passes

1. **Accuracy:** every claim has an owner and source.
2. **Relevance:** the most important evidence is prominent.
3. **Scan:** can an unfamiliar reader identify role, scope, and outcomes quickly?
4. **Depth:** could you explain every highlighted bullet in a technical interview?
5. **Consistency:** do the current draft, evidence ledger, and interview stories agree on ownership, dates, and delivered scope?
6. **Document:** review the actual exported file for layout and readable text, not just Markdown. Identical extracted words can have different visual layouts; neither inspection establishes a particular ATS result.

When using AI, request two alternatives for one bullet, compare them, and confirm the facts before accepting. A polished rewrite that changes scope is a regression.

## Small workflow

Spend one session improving the summary and two important bullets. If earlier versions are available, use them to recover relevant evidence or catch drift rather than restarting the whole document. Preserve the previous version and a short change rationale. Test the revised narrative with a real reader when possible, without treating one reader's stylistic preference as universal hiring law.

Privacy: resumes are intended to disclose selected professional information, not employer secrets or third-party personal data.
