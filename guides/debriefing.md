# Debriefing real interviews: your best prep data

A real interview shows you what no guide or mock can: how the round actually ran, what you were actually asked, and how you actually handled it. The author's view, offered as an opinion and not a measured result, is that your own real interviews are the most valuable data points you have as a candidate. A debrief is how you keep that data: write down what happened while you still remember it, store it where your next session can find it, and let it shape what you prepare next.

You don't need an AI assistant for this. A page of notes, or a conversation with a friend or mentor, works too. An assistant mostly helps you notice what you left out and turn the notes into a next step.

## Why it pays off

- **It's evidence about the real thing.** Practice shows what you can do in practice conditions. A debrief records what happened in real ones: what was asked, where you stalled, and what the interviewer pushed on.
- **It shapes your next step.** Instead of guessing what to study, aim your next session at the part of a real round that went worst, or at a format you now know you'll face.
- **It shows strengths, not only gaps.** Write down what went well so you keep doing it.
- **It adds up.** One debrief is an anecdote. After several, you can see what keeps recurring in your own interviews, such as running out of time at the same kind of step.
- **It's cheap if you do it soon.** Details fade quickly, so a rough ten-minute account the same day is worth more than a careful one a week later.

## When to do it

- **After every real round**, soon after it ends. Short recruiter calls, rounds that went well, rounds that went badly, and processes that end in silence all count.
- **Between rounds of the same loop**, before you prepare for the next one. It's the freshest evidence of how that company's process runs, but a later round can differ, so confirm its format with the recruiter instead of assuming.
- **Again when the outcome arrives.** Add it with the date. One result has many causes you can't see, so don't rewrite your whole approach after a single miss, or decide everything was fine after a single pass.

## What to capture

Write what you can, in any order. The [interview debrief template](../templates/interview-debrief.md) lists these as fields, and the [example](../examples/interview-debrief-example.md) shows one filled in.

- **The format and rules as you experienced them**, and where each came from: written by the recruiter or interviewer, told to you, observed, or assumed. This is where surprises about tools, AI use, or running code show up.
- **What you were asked**, in your own words. The shape of the task or question is enough. Don't try to reproduce confidential or assessment text.
- **What you did:** your approach, the order you worked in, and how you spent the time.
- **Where you got stuck**, and what you did about it.
- **What the interviewer probed, pushed on, or said.** Mark what is a direct quote and what is your paraphrase.
- **What went well, and what surprised you.**
- **What you know versus what you infer.** "They asked three follow-ups about the cache" is something you know. "They thought my design was weak" is a guess about someone else's judgment. Reading an interviewer's face or tone belongs in the second group.
- **What happens next**, as you were told, any feedback you were given and who gave it, and what you still need to ask the recruiter. If no feedback comes, don't read a verdict into the silence.
- **The outcome**, confirmed with a date, or pending with an as-of date. Don't fill it in with a guess.

With only five minutes, write the format and rules, what you were asked, where you got stuck, what surprised you, and one thing to work on next.

## Debrief with an assistant

Say it in any order. You don't need to polish it first. A first message you can paste:

```text
I just finished a [round type] interview for [role]. Help me debrief while it's fresh: [what you were asked, what you did, where you got stuck, what the interviewer pushed on, anything that surprised you]. I'm unsure about: [what you're unsure about]. Keep what I know separate from what I'm guessing, and finish with a short note I can save and one thing to work on next.
```

The coach that matches the round can run it: [technical-screen-coach](../skills/technical-screen-coach/SKILL.md) for coding screens, [ai-assisted-interview-coach](../skills/ai-assisted-interview-coach/SKILL.md) for rounds that allow an assistant, [system-design-coach](../skills/system-design-coach/SKILL.md) for design rounds and project deep dives, and [behavioral-coach](../skills/behavioral-coach/SKILL.md) for stories and follow-ups. The [interview-prep-planner](../skills/interview-prep-planner/SKILL.md) fits when the question is what to do next, for example between rounds. A plain assistant with no skill can run the same debrief from the message above.

A good debrief ends with:

- **A short note you can keep.** You save it yourself, in your prep workspace (below).
- **One thing to practice next.** A coach can write a new task or question with the same shape as the one you got, not a copy of it, so you can redo the part that went wrong.
- **Updates to your other records**, where the round changed them: a story the interviewer probed (your [STAR card](../templates/star-card.md)), a resume claim you couldn't back up (your [experience ledger](../templates/experience-ledger.md)), and the process's row in your [process inventory](../templates/process-inventory.csv). The [field guide](../templates/process-inventory-fields.md) explains the columns.

## Keep it in your prep workspace

Your prep workspace is the private folder, outside any git repository, that the [README](../README.md#keep-your-material-private) suggests for your resume, stories, and practice records. A debrief only pays off if it's there when you start your next session.

- **One note per round**, named by date and round type, such as `2026-03-14-system-design.md`, in a `debriefs` folder or wherever suits you.
- **Update the process inventory** after each round: the furthest stage, the outcome, and when you last confirmed it.
- **Start new sessions from your notes.** An assistant doesn't remember earlier chats unless your tool gives it memory, and then it can still be wrong. Paste your last one or two debriefs, plus a few lines on the patterns so far. If your tool can read files, point it at those notes yourself, since the kit's coaches only read what you paste or explicitly authorize. Notes in your own folder also move with you if you switch assistants.
- **Look across them after a few rounds.** Ask which strengths and gaps repeat and which happened once. With a handful of rounds, a pattern is a guess to test in practice, not a diagnosis.

## Keep it honest and private

- **Your memory is your account.** It isn't a record of what was said or how it was judged. Don't ask an assistant, or yourself, to predict the outcome from how the interview felt.
- **Stay with what happened and what to do next.** "I lost the thread when they asked about failure handling" is useful. "I'm bad at design" isn't, and an assistant shouldn't guess at your state of mind or an interviewer's intent.
- **Don't paste confidential material.** Describe a question's shape, leave out interviewers' names, and generalize employer details. Follow any confidentiality terms you agreed to or rules you were given about the questions.
- **Debrief after, not during.** Don't use an assistant in a live assessment whose rules forbid outside help.
- **Check where your text goes.** It goes to your AI provider, so review your chat history and memory settings before you paste, as the [README](../README.md#keep-your-material-private) explains.
