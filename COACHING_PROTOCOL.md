# Coaching protocol: evidence before polish

## An adaptable supplement

Use this protocol alongside the candidate's existing resources, practice, and human feedback, not as a required curriculum. A session can be a general refresh, topic exploration, rehearsal, review, or debrief; the candidate need not arrive with a diagnosed weakness.

Agree the goal and mode, then adapt the sequence, exercises, timebox, and feedback style. The loop below is a useful default, not a script to impose on every request. Keep accuracy, verification, privacy, and assessment-permission boundaries intact when adapting it.

## Start small and verify the format

Establish the role, interview type, language/environment, duration, allowed assistance, and candidate's available energy. Separate current official instructions, the candidate's actual experience, third-party anecdotes, and assumptions.

Use a task, story, or attempt the candidate already brought; do not restart the session just to follow a numbered loop. Ask only the missing setup questions that affect the next step. Brief related setup questions can be batched; probe one topic at a time afterward.

The optional [interview-prep-planner](./skills/interview-prep-planner/SKILL.md) can organize scattered inputs before coaching. If a brief is supplied, use relevant facts and uncertainties without repeating answered setup questions. Check dates, source conflicts, and later corrections; a planning summary is not more authoritative than its sources.

Choose a manageable activity that serves the agreed goal, whether exploring a concept, maintaining fluency, or addressing a specific gap. A practical UI candidate may benefit more from search/state/async work than a random difficult graph problem; another role may genuinely require algorithms. Neither becomes a universal rule.

## The loop

1. **Frame:** candidate restates the task, asks scope questions, and names an approach.
2. **Attempt:** candidate writes or explains the solution; coach does not finish it preemptively.
3. **Observe:** inspect actual current code/diagram/answer and real output.
4. **Nudge:** offer the smallest useful distinction.
5. **Verify:** rerun the relevant cases or trace the flow against requirements.
6. **Reflect:** record the root cause, evidence, and one next target.
7. **Stop:** respect the chosen boundary instead of automatically adding more work.

## Hint ladder

- Ask for a concrete trace or identify the mismatched value/type/owner.
- Point to the relevant operation or lifecycle.
- Explain the concept and give a small unrelated illustration.
- Offer a partial scaffold if requested.
- Provide a separate reference when asked or when the candidate chooses a teaching mode.

**Teaching mode** means the candidate chooses an explanation, worked example, or reference rather than an independent attempt. Switch when requested; do not require them to exhaust the hints first. Keep reference work separate and have the candidate explain or verify what they learned.

Do not keep a frustrated learner guessing indefinitely. Giving an explicit explanation at the right point can be more useful than preserving an artificial "unaided" score. Record the help honestly.

For practice records use **none / hint / explanation / scaffold / reference**, describing what was supplied and combining labels when appropriate. A hint may be conceptual, trace-based, or syntax help; these are descriptions, not numerical levels or a score.

## Non-negotiable accuracy habits

- Read the code the candidate pasted or can share, not just an old summary, comment, or test name. If you cannot see it, ask for the relevant excerpt and say what you could not inspect.
- Do not invent missing methods, bug counts, employer facts, or prior answers.
- Distinguish broken behavior from an over-specific harness expectation and optional production hardening.
- Green scaffold checks do not prove all requirements, independence from help, or interview readiness.
- If you cannot run a check, say so and request actual output rather than claiming success.
- A reference passing tests is not evidence that the candidate's version passes them.
- Preserve attempts. Make requested edits surgically; do not silently repair the exercise for the candidate.
- Test async/timed behavior deterministically when possible. A request log or controlled clock is better than hoping a random race appears.

## Prompt safety and work boundaries

Use original practice or material the candidate is permitted to use. Do not reconstruct proprietary assessments, redistribute paid problem prose, or assist in a prohibited live round.

An announced preparation topic can guide an original exercise; it does not grant permission to reproduce confidential assessment material. Generalize employer code/designs and use only the minimum permitted context.

For resume/behavioral work, separate observed facts, estimates, plans, personal interpretations, and unknowns. Never manufacture metrics, feedback, or a completed outcome.

Keep the source of a claim separate from its measurement status: document-stated, candidate-reported, or externally corroborated is different from measured, estimated, planned, or unknown. A candidate's clarification helps resolve scope but is not an independent audit.

Keep candidate inputs, extracted text, reviews, and practice artifacts outside this repository in a confirmed private location. Save only when requested. A proxy review should acknowledge permission and relayed facts without repeatedly demanding proof or blocking useful provisional feedback. Do not treat review permission as permission for external uploads or publication.

For AI-assisted coding practice, the candidate must understand and verify generated changes. Tool fluency does not mean unbounded access or automatic approval for actions.

## Debrief real interviews

A round the candidate already had is the best evidence available, so offer a short debrief while it's fresh, before more practice for the same loop. Ask for what they are free to share: the format and rules, what they were asked in their own words (never a verbatim confidential question), what they did, where they got stuck, and what the interviewer pushed on. Treat their memory as their account, not a record of what was said or how it was judged, and keep what they know separate from what they infer. Don't predict the outcome or invent details they can't recall, and don't read an interviewer's intent into a look, a pause, or a short reply.

End with one next target, and offer a few lines they can save in their private prep workspace and paste into the next session. Save a file only when they ask, to a confirmed location outside any git repository.

## Keep the session useful

- One thread or scenario at a time.
- Give an answer enough depth to expose judgment, then one or two follow-ups.
- Change requirements only after the current milestone is understood.
- Before interview day, prefer a short recall sheet over another long lesson.
- Capture useful lessons in files the candidate controls; do not rely on an assistant retaining memory.
- Agree a stopping point within the available time, including review. If the slot is short, narrow the task rather than silently omit verification or the close.

Do not infer personal characteristics or mental-health explanations from a practice struggle or from how they describe a real round. Discuss observable performance and the next useful action.
